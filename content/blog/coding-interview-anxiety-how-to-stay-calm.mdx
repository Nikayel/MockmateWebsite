The fastest thing that helps is slowing your breathing out, not in: a few rounds where the exhale is clearly longer than the inhale, done in the minutes before you join the call. The second fastest is having a sentence ready for the moment your mind goes blank, so you are not composing one while panicking. Everything else in this post is about making those two moments rarer, by removing the parts of an interview that are unfamiliar rather than by trying to feel less afraid.

Nothing here is treatment, and none of it is a substitute for talking to a clinician if anxiety is affecting your life beyond interviews.

## Why coding interviews are unusually good at triggering anxiety

Technical interviews stack most of the things that reliably raise stress into one hour.

| Stressor | Why it lands |
|----------|--------------|
| Evaluation | Someone is judging you, and the verdict is binary |
| Time pressure | A visible countdown against an unknown amount of work |
| Dual task | You have to think and narrate at the same time |
| Uncertainty | You do not know which problem is coming |
| Stakes | Comp, visa timing, or how long the search continues |
| Audience | Another person watches you struggle in real time |

The mechanism that matters for performance is narrow: acute stress eats working memory, and working memory is exactly the resource an algorithm question runs on. Holding a pointer, an invariant and a loop bound in your head at once is cheap when calm and expensive when not. That is why the failure feels like forgetting rather than like fear. You have not lost the knowledge, you have lost the scratch space you use to assemble it. That is also what separates this from the slower problem of [forgetting problems you actually solved](/blog/why-you-forget-leetcode-problems), which is a review problem rather than a pressure one.

## A little pressure helps, a lot does not

The inverted-U relationship between arousal and performance goes back to Robert Yerkes and John Dodson in 1908, and it is worth knowing as a shape rather than as a law. The original work was narrower than the popular version of it and the curve has been criticized as a simplification ever since. But the practical read holds up:

```
Performance
    ^
    |      /\
    |     /  \
    |    /    \
    |   /      \
    |  /        \
    | /          \
    |/            \
    +--------------> Arousal
   Low   Optimal   High
```

Flat calm is not the goal, and candidates who feel nothing tend to miss things. The goal is moving left from overload toward alert. Most of the techniques below are ways of doing that, and almost all of them work by reducing uncertainty rather than by reducing feeling.

## Physical symptoms and what to do with them

Your body is running a threat response. It is not wrong, it is just aimed at the wrong problem.

| Symptom | What is happening | What to do |
|---------|-------------------|------------|
| Racing heart | Sympathetic arousal | Slow the breath, exhale longer than you inhale |
| Sweaty palms | A stress response, not overheating | Dry your hands, then ignore it |
| Dry mouth | Blood flow shifted away from digestion | Keep water within reach and use it |
| Shaky voice | Muscle tension | Speak slower and pause between sentences rather than pushing through |
| Blank mind | Working memory is loaded | Say your prepared sentence out loud, then restate the problem |

The last row is the one to rehearse. Saying something removes the silence that is making it worse, and restating the problem reloads the details you just dropped.

## Techniques for the five minutes before

**Slow your breathing out.** Any pattern with a longer exhale than inhale will do. The commonly taught version is four counts in, hold for seven, out for eight, repeated a few times. The counts are a scaffold, not the mechanism. If holding for seven feels like extra work, drop the hold and just make the out-breath longer than the in-breath.

**Set the room up so nothing surprises you.** Water within reach, phone silenced, notifications off, the editor or platform already open and tested, a second tab closed rather than tempting. Every small unknown you remove is one you will not spend attention on later.

**Rehearse the opening thirty seconds.** Your introduction and your first move on any problem should both be automatic: restate the problem in your own words, ask about input size and edge cases, say what you are going to try before you type. Making the opening automatic means the expensive part of your attention is still available when the problem gets hard.

**Reframe what the hour is for.** The interviewer is not looking for reasons to reject you. Filling a role is expensive and slow, and the person on the call generally wants the search to end. Replacing "they are deciding if I am good enough" with "I am showing how I work through something" is not positive thinking, it is a more accurate description of what a technical round measures.

## Techniques for during the interview

**Prepared sentences for silence.** Silence feels much longer to the person being evaluated than to the person watching. Have these ready so the gap is filled with reasoning instead of dread:

- "Let me restate the problem to make sure I have it."
- "I want to think about the edge cases before I commit to an approach."
- "I am weighing a hash map against sorting first, here is the tradeoff."
- "Let me trace a small example by hand."

**Narrate the tradeoff, not the panic.** The interviewer is scoring how you think. A sentence about why you chose one structure over another is worth more than another line of code, and it buys you thinking time at the same time.

**Ground yourself for ten seconds when it spikes.** Feet on the floor, hands on the keyboard, one slow breath. It is not a cure, it interrupts the loop long enough to say your next sentence.

**Ask.** Asking whether a constraint holds, or whether the interviewer wants the optimal solution before the working one, is normal engineering behavior and it is scored as such. Candidates who suffer most are usually the ones treating the round as an exam where questions are forbidden.

## What to do after you get stuck

Getting stuck is expected. It is the part of the hour the interviewer learns most from, and it is the part almost nobody rehearses. Have a sequence ready so being stuck does not also mean being lost.

1. **Say it out loud.** "I do not immediately see the efficient approach, so let me work from what I do know." This is not a confession. It tells the interviewer where you are, which is the only way they can help you, and it stops the spiral where you go quiet and then panic about having gone quiet.
2. **Fall back to brute force and say so.** State the naive solution and its complexity, then say you want to improve it. You now have a working answer on the board and a direction, instead of nothing.
3. **Shrink the input.** Solve it by hand for three elements. Many stuck moments break here, because the pattern is visible at a size your head can hold.
4. **Say what you have ruled out.** "Sorting first does not help because I need the original indices" is a scoring sentence. It shows the search was structured rather than random.
5. **Take the hint.** If the interviewer offers a nudge, take it, say thank you, and keep going. A candidate who uses a hint well reads as collaborative. A candidate who refuses one reads as difficult to work with.
6. **Reset the clock in your head.** Being stuck for four minutes feels like fifteen. Check the actual time before concluding the round is lost, because a round is often recoverable long after it stops feeling that way.

Then let it go. A costly and very common mistake is spending the second problem replaying the first one.

## A preparation routine that actually lowers anxiety

Preparation reduces anxiety when it removes uncertainty, and does very little when it just adds volume. A routine worth running looks like this.

**Four weeks out.** Find out the format from the recruiter, and ask directly: how many rounds, how long, which language, shared editor or their platform, whether tests run. Recruiters generally answer this, and it is worth asking. Then map your weak patterns rather than your favorite ones.

**Two weeks out.** Practice out loud, every time, even alone. Narrating while coding is a separate skill from coding and it is the one that collapses first under pressure. If you have only ever solved problems in silence, the interview will be the first time you attempt both at once.

**One week out.** Do full-length timed rounds rather than single problems. Fatigue and pacing are what get people at minute forty, and you cannot rehearse either in ten-minute chunks. Write your behavioral stories down once so they are not being composed live.

**Two days out.** Stop learning new material. Rerun patterns you already know so recall stays warm. Cramming a new topic the night before mostly buys you the feeling of unpreparedness in a fresh area.

**The day of.** Light review only, food, water, and the environment set up an hour early rather than five minutes early.

## How mock practice helps, and what it does not do

Familiarity is the honest mechanism here. The tenth time you do something is less alarming than the first, because more of it has become predictable: the shape of the opening, what silence feels like, how it feels to be wrong in front of someone and recover. Mock rounds move interview conditions from unfamiliar to routine.

Two limits worth being straight about. The number of reps is not a dose with a guaranteed effect, and anyone quoting you one is making it up. And mock practice targets situational nerves. If your anxiety extends well past interviews, into sleep, health, or ordinary work, that is a different problem and a clinician is the right person to talk to about it, not a practice platform.

What you can control is how much of the hour is new. A round where the format, the narration and the recovery script are all familiar leaves you with only the actual problem to be nervous about, which is a much smaller thing.

## Practicing with the pressure dialed down

If the interview environment itself is what spikes you, practice with parts of it removed and add them back as they stop mattering. CodeSparring has three toggles built for this, all of them in-session:

- **Calm mode** mutes the interface colors and turns the sound effects off.
- **Focus mode** hides the non-essential panels so there is one thing on screen, and turns calm mode on with it.
- **Hide timer** removes the countdown, which is a major pressure source for many people, while the session still runs normally.

Use them early, then turn them off one at a time. The point is not a permanently gentler environment, it is arriving at the full one having already done the hard part somewhere quieter. You can also choose the difficulty, so the first rep does not have to be a hard graph problem.

You will still get a scored breakdown covering communication and problem solving, which matters here more than it sounds: it is easy to walk away certain you communicated badly when the transcript says otherwise, and evidence about what actually happened is worth more than the memory of how it felt.

## Interview day checklist

**24 hours before**

- [ ] Light review only, no new topics
- [ ] Behavioral stories written down
- [ ] Environment tested: editor, camera, microphone, connection
- [ ] Sleep prioritized over one more problem

**1 hour before**

- [ ] Eat something light
- [ ] Water within reach
- [ ] A few slow breaths with a longer exhale than inhale
- [ ] Reread your prepared sentences for silence and for being stuck

**5 minutes before**

- [ ] Close every tab you do not need
- [ ] Phone silenced and out of sight
- [ ] One slow breath
- [ ] Remind yourself the hour is for showing how you work

**During**

- [ ] Restate the problem before touching the keyboard
- [ ] Ask about input size and edge cases
- [ ] Narrate tradeoffs, not panic
- [ ] Brute force first if you are stuck, then improve it
- [ ] Take hints and keep moving

## The part that gets better

Feeling nervous before a technical interview is a normal response to being evaluated, and it does not predict how the round goes. What separates candidates who cope from candidates who freeze is usually not calmness, it is having a plan for the bad minute: a sentence for the blank moment, a fallback for being stuck, and enough reps that the format itself is boring.

Practicing the recovery is the highest-value thing on that list, because it is the only one nobody rehearses. When you are next practicing, get deliberately stuck on a problem and work the sequence above out loud instead of skipping to the answer.
